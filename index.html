What I'd bring

Measuring progress, then changing the plan

The habit I'd bring

Every learner I work with has a documented plan, recorded progress against it, and a plan that gets revised when the record shows it isn’t working. Behavioral data collected session by session, IEP goals tracked and adjusted, instruction changed on the evidence rather than on a hunch. Most corporate training is delivered once and never measured — this is the discipline that fixes that, and I’ve been held to it professionally for years.

Explaining the same thing more than one way

Special education background

Special education teaches you that one explanation is rarely enough. The same idea often has to be rebuilt — shown as well as said, broken into steps, sequenced differently — before it lands. To be clear about what this is and isn’t: it isn’t matching people to “learning styles”, which the research has not supported. It’s giving everyone more than one route into the same idea, which it has.

Disability support, formally certified

Registered Behavior Technician

The RBT credential is a formal certification in supporting people with disabilities — applied behavior analysis delivered one-to-one, to standard, with the data collection and supervision that comes with it. Combined with years of IEP work, that makes designing for disability something I can actually document, rather than just say.

Why these methods — the evidence

None of this is mine. These are established practices in education, and I lead with them for one reason: the research behind them is unusually strong, and they’re often the parts workplace training leaves out. The sources are public, and I’d rather point at them than ask anyone to take my word for it.

Checking whether it worked, then changing the plan

Formative assessment · Black & Wiliam

The review behind Inside the Black Box found effect sizes of roughly 0.4 to 0.7 — larger than most known educational interventions. The same review noted that doing it well is relatively rare in practice. That gap is most of why I keep bringing it up.

Deciding from the data, not the hunch

Data-based decision making · IES / What Works Clearinghouse

The U.S. Department of Education’s Institute of Education Sciences publishes a practice guide on using student achievement data to support instructional decisions, and multi-tiered support models are built around reviewing progress data and then adjusting grouping, intensity or focus. That review-and-adjust cycle is the habit I’d bring to training.

The behavioral work

Applied behavior analysis · meta-analytic support

ABA is a designated evidence-based practice with decades of study behind it; meta-analyses report moderate to high effectiveness across most measured outcome domains. My RBT work was delivering it to protocol, with the data collection and supervision that requires.

More than one route into an idea

Multiple representations · not learning styles

Worth separating carefully, because the two get confused. Matching instruction to a person’s “learning style” has been tested repeatedly and not supported — it’s generally treated as a myth now. Presenting the same idea in more than one form, words alongside visuals, is supported. I do the second one.
